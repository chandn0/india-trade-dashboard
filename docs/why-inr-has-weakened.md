# Why Has the Indian Rupee Fallen So Much?

_Interpretive note using the project’s merchandise-trade and INR/USD series._
_Prepared June 2026. Fiscal year = April–March. Exchange rate = INR per USD, so a higher number means a weaker rupee._

---

## Short answer

The rupee has weakened for a **combination** of reasons, not one single reason:

1. India has run a **persistent merchandise trade deficit**, so the economy structurally needs more foreign currency for imports than it earns from goods exports.
2. India is still meaningfully dependent on **dollar-priced imports** such as crude oil, gold, electronics, semiconductors, and other industrial inputs.
3. Over long periods, India has usually had **higher inflation than the US**, which tends to produce gradual nominal depreciation.
4. The rupee is also shaped by **global dollar strength, capital flows, and risk sentiment**, not just trade.
5. The RBI generally **manages volatility rather than fixing the exchange rate**, so long-run pressure shows up as stepwise depreciation rather than a perfectly flat currency.

So the honest reading is: **the trade deficit is one structural pressure on INR, but it is not the whole story.**

---

## What the project data shows

From the stored fiscal-year FX series:

- FY 2010-11 average: **₹45.6 per USD**
- FY 2025-26 average: **₹88.3 per USD**
- FY 2025-26 year-end: **₹94.7 per USD**

That means the rupee weakened by about **1.9x** over 15 fiscal years, or roughly **4.5% CAGR** in average INR/USD.

From the merchandise-trade series over the same broad period:

- FY 2010-11 goods deficit: **$120.0 bn**
- FY 2020-21 goods deficit: **$102.6 bn**
- FY 2025-26 goods deficit: **$333.2 bn**
- Export coverage ratio fell from **67.6%** in FY 2010-11 to **57.0%** in FY 2025-26

So the long-run picture is clear: **the rupee weakened while the merchandise deficit remained persistent and later widened sharply.**

---

## Why the rupee weakened

### 1. Persistent goods deficits create structural FX demand

India has run a merchandise deficit in every year of the project window. That matters because imports have to be paid for in foreign currency, mostly dollars.

When imports grow faster than exports:

- domestic buyers need more dollars to pay overseas suppliers
- exporters do not bring in enough dollars to fully offset that demand
- the economy becomes more dependent on services exports, remittances, and capital inflows to fill the gap

That does **not** mean the rupee must fall every single year because of trade alone. It does mean the rupee lives with a **constant structural headwind**.

---

### 2. India imports many things that are hard to avoid

The rupee is more vulnerable when a country imports products that are both large and difficult to substitute quickly.

In this project’s trade data, the biggest examples are:

- **petroleum crude and products**
- **gold / precious metals**
- **electronics**
- **integrated circuits / chips**
- other industrial inputs tied to manufacturing and energy use

These categories matter because they are either:

- essential to the economy,
- linked to consumer demand that does not disappear easily, or
- part of supply chains where India is still import-dependent.

That is why a weaker rupee can become self-reinforcing: the country needs dollars to buy imports, and when the rupee falls, those imports become costlier in local currency.

---

### 3. Higher inflation than the US tends to show up as gradual depreciation

Over long periods, currencies often adjust to differences in inflation.

If India’s domestic prices and costs rise faster than those in the United States, a perfectly stable INR/USD rate would slowly make Indian goods and wages more expensive in dollar terms. In practice, part of that adjustment often comes through **nominal rupee depreciation**.

This is one reason rupee weakness should not be read only as crisis. Some part of it is the ordinary long-run adjustment of a faster-growing, higher-inflation emerging economy.

---

### 4. The dollar itself has strong global cycles

The rupee is priced against the US dollar, not against a neutral measuring stick.

So INR can weaken because:

- the **dollar strengthens globally**
- US interest rates rise and capital moves toward dollar assets
- global risk appetite falls and investors prefer safer markets
- commodity prices rise, increasing import bills for energy importers

In other words, even if India’s domestic story were unchanged, INR/USD could still move because the **USD side of the pair** moved.

---

### 5. Capital flows help finance the gap, but they are not perfectly stable

A country can run a goods deficit for a long time if it receives offsetting inflows such as:

- services exports
- remittances
- foreign direct investment
- portfolio inflows
- external borrowing

India benefits from several of these buffers, especially services and remittances. That is why the merchandise deficit does **not** translate one-for-one into currency collapse.

But these financing sources can be cyclical. If capital inflows slow or become more expensive while the import bill stays high, pressure on INR increases.

---

### 6. RBI policy smooths the path, but does not erase the trend

The RBI can lean against sharp currency moves using reserves and liquidity management. That often reduces panic and smooths volatility.

But smoothing is not the same as permanently fixing the rupee. If the underlying forces remain:

- persistent external deficits,
- import dependence,
- inflation differentials,
- and periodic dollar strength,

then the exchange rate usually adjusts over time anyway.

That is why the rupee often looks like it weakens in **steps** rather than in one continuous straight line.

---

## Why the cumulative-deficit chart looks so strongly correlated with INR

This part is important.

The chart can create a **stronger visual correlation than the true causal relationship** because:

1. **Cumulative deficit mostly moves in one direction:** up.
2. **INR/USD also trends upward over long periods:** a higher INR/USD number means a weaker rupee.

When two series both trend upward with time, they can look tightly linked even when one is only **part** of the explanation.

So the chart is useful as a visual of shared long-run pressure, but it should **not** be read as:

> cumulative merchandise deficit mechanically causes rupee depreciation in a one-to-one way.

A more careful interpretation is:

- the merchandise deficit is a **structural pressure**
- the cumulative version makes the relationship look visually tighter
- actual yearly rupee moves also depend on inflation, oil, capital flows, and global USD cycles

---

## What the data suggests about the recent period

The most worrying part of the current window is not just that the rupee weakened. It is **why**.

Between FY 2023-24 and FY 2025-26:

- exports stayed broadly flat around **$437-442 bn**
- imports rose from about **$678 bn** to **$775 bn**
- the goods deficit widened from about **$241 bn** to **$333 bn**
- average INR/USD moved from **₹82.8** to **₹88.3**

That combination is more uncomfortable than a deficit widening driven only by a short oil spike. It suggests a more structural mismatch: **import demand kept rising while export growth stalled.**

---

## What should not be concluded

This note should **not** be read as saying:

- “the rupee fell only because of the trade deficit”
- “every rupee depreciation is a sign of economic failure”
- “a weaker rupee is always bad for exports”

Some depreciation is a normal feature of a developing economy with higher inflation and periodic external shocks. The problem becomes more serious when depreciation is paired with:

- weak export growth,
- rising import dependence,
- and heavier reliance on volatile external financing.

---

## Bottom line

The rupee has fallen so much because India faces a **long-running external-balance problem**, not because of one bad year.

The merchandise deficit matters a lot, especially because it is tied to large, sticky, dollar-priced imports. But the rupee’s long decline is really the combined result of:

- persistent goods deficits,
- import dependence,
- higher domestic inflation than the US,
- global dollar cycles,
- and changing capital flows.

That is also why the cumulative-deficit chart should be read carefully: it is pointing at a **real structural pressure**, but it visually overstates how simple the causal story is.
